Ask your way around.

Whereabouts does two things. Venues: at a conference, a clinic, a campus building that someone has mapped, you join, say where you are, and ask: "How do I get to room 204?" You're told the way in words, step by step, with no camera and no signal needed. Rooms: at home, it knows where everything is, down to what's inside each drawer, and walks you to it.

Get it on your iPhone. Asking your way around a venue, and finding things in a room, work on any iPhone with iOS 17 or later; mapping a floor or a room needs a Pro model (iPhone 12 Pro or newer). Whereabouts is in open testing through Apple's TestFlight: install the free TestFlight app from the App Store, then open the link below on the phone and tap Install. No invite code is needed. Venues: at a conference or a venue

  1. Someone maps it. The organizers' own people walk each floor once with an iPhone Pro, naming rooms and landmarks as they go: the elevators, registration, room 204, booth 42. A floor takes a morning, not a contractor.
  2. You join and ask. Join the event with its six-letter code, or the link or door code the organizers put up. Say where you are ("I'm at the elevators") and ask the way: "How do I get to room 204?", "Where's booth 42?" You're told the way in words, one stretch at a time: "Walk straight about 12 meters, past the coffee station on your right, then turn left." Say next, repeat, or I'm there. No camera, and it works with the venue's network down. If you want the last few meters guided by the camera, that's a tap away.
  3. It stays true. Every arrival checks the map again; when something isn't where the map says, the organizers hear about it, and the map tells you when it was last checked. Exhibitors can be listed by the name people ask for.

Rooms: at home

  1. Scan a room. Walk slowly around it, about a minute. The phone measures the walls, doors and furniture, spots everyday things as you go, and Claude names everything in the best photos. Open a drawer or a closet and tap Look inside: every spoon and shirt in it is listed too, pinned where it lies. Say what things are called at home ("Mom's chair") and the map keeps your word for it.
  2. Ask where anything is. Say it or pick it from the list: "Where are my keys?" You hear the answer at once, down to which drawer, and on the phone's screen or the computer's floor plan.
  3. Be walked to it. Turn-by-turn directions around the furniture and through doorways, a warning before anything in the way, and in the last half meter your hand is guided to the thing itself.
  4. Keep it current. Each room opens as a floor plan and in 3D in the editor on a computer, where the people you share with rename things, add notes, fix the plan and share rooms. When a shelf changes, scan that spot again instead of the whole room. When something can't be found, whoever keeps the room hears about it.

Who it's for

  • If you're blind or have low vision. Everything works by voice and with VoiceOver: ask, hear the way or where a thing is, be guided there, read a label. Whereabouts was built with blind users first, and that's still where it's deepest.
  • If you run a conference or a venue. Accessibility you can show, not promise: your people map the floors, and every attendee with an iPhone can ask their way around from the first morning. Exhibitors get found by name.
  • If you look after someone who forgets. One person keeps the rooms scanned and the names right; the other just asks. You hear when something isn't where the map says, and a month-old map gets a reminder.
  • If you lose things. Keys, passport, the good scissors, the charger that lives in a drawer: ask, and hear which drawer. The map doesn't forget where you put things, even when you do.

Good to know

  • Guidance works alongside a white cane or a guide dog, not instead of them. Directions in a venue come from the map as it was scanned; camera guidance warns about what the camera sees, and both can miss things.
  • Your rooms and photos are yours: the privacy policy says exactly what's kept and who handles it, and how to delete it all from inside the app. A photo with a person in it is never kept. A floor published for an event is meant to be found: its map and location are open to everyone who joins.
